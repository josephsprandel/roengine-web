Zeno

Live · you choose how close is close enough · made by Claude

Zeno of Elea, about 450 BC: to reach the finish line you first have to cover half the distance. Then half of what's left. Then half of that. There is always another half. So how does anyone ever arrive?

Below, a runner does exactly that on a hundred-yard field. Every time it covers half of what's left, the view zooms in two times, so the rest of the race looks exactly like the start. The ticks double in speed with every halving and never seem to get faster. The tone climbs an octave with every halving and never gets higher. Those are two old sound illusions, Risset's rhythm and Shepard's tone, and they are the paradox, in sound.

It never reaches the line exactly, and it doesn't have to. Pick any tolerance and it gets inside it, then stays inside. Pick a tighter one and it just takes more halvings: twelve for an inch, about forty for a hydrogen atom, a hundred and twenty-three for the Planck length, the smallest distance physics can talk about. That is the whole modern answer to Zeno, and it's all "equals" means when mathematicians say 1 + ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + … = 2. It took about two thousand years to say it clearly, in the 1800s, and it's the question every machinist already asks: plus or minus what?

At what speed do you stop?

Joe, who spent years as a precision machinist before he owned a repair shop, likes to ask people this: if your speedometer had infinite accuracy, reading to millionths of a mile an hour and beyond, at what speed do you stop?

60.000000000000mph
Braking evenly from 60 mph to a stop in five seconds.

Zero. And there is no last speed before zero where the stopping happens. Name any speed, however small, a millionth of a mile an hour, a trillionth, and the car was going exactly that fast at one instant before it stopped. Between any speed and zero there's always another, smaller one. The car passes through every one of them, infinitely many, in a finite time: the same trick as the runner.

A real speedometer shows a last number before zero only because it has a tolerance. The screen above redraws about sixty times a second, so its "last speed" is just wherever it happened to redraw. Slow motion shows the other side: every halving of the speed takes half as long as the one before, so infinitely many of them fit inside the last second, and the car still stops on time.
